Abstract

Room impulse responses (RIRs) describe how sound propagates between a source and a listener, and underlie applications ranging from immersive audio to speech enhancement and recognition. RIRs are typically obtained from hybrid acoustic simulators, which combine wave solvers with geometric methods; this is accurate but slow and computationally demanding. We show that generative models can take the place of such a simulator: given a mesh of the room and the source and listener positions, our method CHOIR produces a high-fidelity RIR in a single network evaluation. To reduce ambiguity in this ill-posed problem, a few RIRs measured at different locations in the same room are provided as additional inputs. CHOIR is trained across many rooms via flow matching, and on AcousticRooms it improves reverberation time, clarity, and early-decay time over prior methods in both seen and unseen rooms. We also show results on sim2real transfer to real rooms, and on a downstream speech recognition task, where speech rendered with CHOIR's RIRs gives error rates closest to those obtained with the ground-truth RIRs.

Generative models as surrogate simulators

Most applications need RIRs in large numbers, and to meet that demand they are typically simulated using hybrid simulators. Given a mesh of the room, the material properties of its surfaces, and the source and receiver positions, these simulators combine full wave solvers at low frequencies with geometric methods at higher frequencies. Although accurate, they are slow and expensive. We ask whether a generative model can be used as quick and cheap surrogates for such simulators.

Ill-posedness

During the simulation process, the simulator is also given information about the surface materials. In practice these are rarely known, and without them the same mesh and the same source and receiver positions admit many different responses (the animation below demonstrates this behavior). A predictor model trained to predict a single answer would average over all these possibilities creating rather a "blurred" prediction. CHOIR instead, samples from the conditional distribution of the possbile RIRs.

Flow matching

We sample with flow matching, which transports a distribution that is easy to draw from onto the target by learning a time-dependent velocity field. The velocity that induces the transport is intractable, but regressing the conditional velocities along paths between paired noise and data samples recovers it. To generate, we draw a noise sample and integrate the field forward until it lands on the data. CHOIR takes one Euler step, so a response costs a single network evaluation. In the animations below, we show both the training and inference processes in the standard settings.

CHOIR

RIRs are high-dimensional and non-smooth in time, so learning a flow over them directly is difficult. We build it in a compact space instead. A variational autoencoder first compresses the response in the complex STFT domain, trained with losses on the multi-resolution spectrum, the energy decay and the early part of the waveform, so the latent retains how the signal decays and when its reflections arrive. It is then frozen, and a conditional flow is trained in that space with a diffusion transformer, as the diagram sets out. The context is a panoramic depth map at the receiver, the source positions, and K reference RIRs measured elsewhere in the room; unlike FLAC we group each reference into a single token, so what the model learns from one stays tied to where it was measured.

Real-world rooms

We fine-tune both FLAC and CHOIR on Hearing Anything Anywhere, four measured rooms. The setup is mirrored with respect to training: the reference RIRs share a common source instead of a receiver, and the depth map is captured at the source. The figure varies how many measured RIRs each model is fine-tuned on. As that budget grows CHOIR improves on all three metrics, and at 36 it passes both FLAC and the nearest neighbor, with an 11.0% lower T60 and a 21.6% lower EDT.

Speech recognition

We convolve each RIR in both splits with four LibriSpeech recordings and transcribe them with Parakeet and Qwen3-ASR, adding pink noise and multi-talker babble at five SNR levels. The two tables report word error rate under each noise type. Both CHOIR and FLAC score a better rate than the ground truth, FLAC the lowest of the three. Our goal is to imitate the simulator, so we seek the model closest to the ground truth rather than the lowest; CHOIR is closer in almost all cases, and on average 25% closer.

Each example is the same sentence heard through the ground-truth RIR, through ours and through FLAC's, with what the model transcribed marked word by word. A faithful RIR should reproduce the errors the ground truth causes rather than avoid them.
